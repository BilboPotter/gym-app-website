The best beginner workout app is the one that solves the part of training you are least ready to manage: choosing a plan, learning movements, logging the session, or deciding how to progress.

No single app is best at all four. A large workout library can be useful if you want to choose sessions yourself. A structured strength app is better if you want today’s exercises decided before you reach the gym. A voice-led trainer may suit someone who does not want to look at a screen between sets.

How this comparison was made: Maatriks Team reviewed official product pages, help centers, and US store listings on July 29, 2026. We did not treat company marketing claims as hands-on test results. Prices can change and vary by platform, promotion, and region.

Maatriks publishes this comparison and is one of the products covered below.

What a beginner workout app should make easier

Before comparing names, decide which jobs you want the app to handle:

  • Planning: does it give you an organized workout, or only tools for building one?
  • Exercise guidance: can you see how a movement is performed without leaving the session?
  • Gym logging: can you record warm-ups, working sets, weight, reps, and time quickly?
  • Progression: does it use a clear rule, an algorithm, a coach-like conversation, or manual decisions?
  • Control: can you edit the plan when equipment, preferences, or experience change?

A beginner does not necessarily need the app with the most exercises or data. It should reduce uncertainty without hiding what you are being asked to do.

Fitbod: generated workout recommendations

Fitbod is designed around personalized workout recommendations and a large exercise system. Its official help center says premium access includes generating workouts from historical data, exercise tracking, and progress insights. That can be useful when your main problem is deciding what to train.

The tradeoff is that you are trusting a recommendation system with more of the planning. Beginners who prefer a smaller, repeatable strength program may find a narrower program easier to understand.

Fitbod’s official subscription help currently lists a seven-day trial, USD 15.99 per month, or USD 95.99 per year. It also warns that prices can vary by region, promotion, and platform.

StrongLifts: a structured strength system

StrongLifts is still closely associated with 5×5 training, but the current app is broader than a single rigid template. Its official product page describes multiple programs and templates, custom exercises, time-based exercises, exercise videos, automatic progression, deloads, rest timers, and workout logging.

That makes it a strong fit when you want the program and progression rules to be central. It is less suitable if you want broad class-style workouts or a conversational coach.

StrongLifts’ official support page gives example pricing of USD 11.99 monthly or USD 59.99 yearly and says the annual plan includes a seven-day trial. The amount shown to an individual user can vary.

Ray: voice-guided training

Ray takes a different approach. Its App Store description emphasizes voice-guided strength training, conversational adjustments, Apple Watch and Apple Health integration, and hands-free counting for supported exercises.

This can fit someone who wants the phone to stay out of their hands during the session. Because several store purchase rows use the same generic “Premium Access” label, it would be misleading to turn any one listed amount into a universal monthly price. Check the plan and renewal period in the app before subscribing.

Hevy: gym logging first

Hevy is built around planning routines, recording lifts, reviewing charts, and sharing workouts. Its current official listing includes exercise videos, custom exercises, rest timers, warm-up and working-set types, measurements, and Apple Watch support.

Hevy Trainer adds generated programming for Pro users. Hevy’s own help center makes an important distinction: the program generator uses an algorithm rather than AI. It also describes progressive-overload suggestions and performance reporting.

The US App Store currently lists common Hevy Pro options at USD 2.99 per month, USD 23.99 per year, and USD 74.99 for lifetime access. Other platforms or regions may show different amounts.

Nike Training Club: free guided workouts

Nike Training Club is a useful starting point when you want guided workouts rather than a detailed strength log. Nike describes more than 200 free workouts and programs, with video instruction and options across strength, mobility, yoga, and conditioning.

The key distinction is workflow. It is a broad guided-workout library, not a per-working-set Auto-progression system. That may be exactly right if variety and follow-along instruction matter more than detailed barbell tracking.

FitnessAI: algorithmic progression

FitnessAI explicitly markets algorithmic weight and repetition recommendations. Its official pages describe progressive overload, scheduling, equipment inputs, and recommendations based on logged training.

This is a fit for someone who wants the recommendation engine to do more of the target-setting. The current official web offer advertises USD 89 per year, while App Store purchase options show multiple tiers that may reflect platform, promotion, or legacy plans.

Maatriks: a prepared plan with visible rules

Maatriks is designed to give a beginner a clear starting program, show the movement, and keep the workout log on one screen. You can track warm-ups, working sets, kilograms, reps, timed exercises, swaps, and exercises added during the session.

Progression is optional and deterministic. When Auto-progression is enabled, eligible completed working sets move one rep at a time inside their saved per-set ranges. Load increases only after a set reaches the top of its range. Warm-ups, timed sets, and ineligible movements stay outside that automatic path.

AI has a different job: it produces post-workout feedback. It does not decide the program or progression. The current build is free and Maatriks is available from the App Store and Google Play.

Which one should you choose?

  • Choose Fitbod if broad generated recommendations are the main value you want.
  • Choose StrongLifts if a structured strength system and automatic progression are the priority.
  • Choose Ray if voice interaction and a hands-off session matter most.
  • Choose Hevy if you want a polished social gym tracker and routine builder.
  • Choose Nike Training Club if you want free guided workouts across many styles.
  • Choose FitnessAI if algorithmic load and rep recommendations are the main product you are buying.
  • Choose Maatriks if you want a prepared, editable plan with in-workout guidance and progression rules you can see.

If you are still unsure, start with the workflow rather than the feature list. Do you want to follow a guided session, run a repeatable strength program, or record a plan you already understand? The answer usually narrows the list quickly.
