Your first week at the gym needs a short plan, manageable weights, and enough repetition to learn the movements. Three full-body sessions are a practical place to start.

The goal is not to exhaust yourself or test your limits. It is to leave knowing what each exercise feels like and with a record you can use next time.

Before you begin: This is general training information, not individual medical advice. If an exercise causes sharp, severe, or persistent pain, stop and seek qualified help. Ask gym staff or a qualified coach for hands-on setup guidance when you are unsure.

Three rules for the first week

  1. Start lighter than your ego suggests. Choose a load you can control and finish with several clean repetitions still available.
  2. Learn a small number of movements. Repeating the same basic patterns is more useful than trying twenty machines once.
  3. Write down what happened. Record the load, completed reps, and any setup issue you want to remember.

Your simple three-day plan

Train on non-consecutive days if possible—for example Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Use one or two light preparation sets before a loaded movement when you need them.

Day 1

  • Leg press — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Machine chest press — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Lat pulldown — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Seated leg curl — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Hand plank — 2 timed sets of 20–30 seconds

Day 2

  • Goblet squat to a comfortable depth — 2 working sets of 8–10 reps
  • Seated cable row — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Dumbbell bench press — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Machine or dumbbell Romanian deadlift pattern — 2 working sets of 8–10 reps
  • Farmer carry — 2 short, controlled trips

Day 3

  • Leg press — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Machine shoulder press — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Assisted pull-up or lat pulldown — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Hip thrust machine or glute bridge — 2 working sets of 8–12 reps
  • Hand plank — 2 timed sets of 20–30 seconds

You do not need to force a movement that feels wrong or cannot be set up safely. Use an available alternative for the same general pattern and record the change.

How to choose the starting weight

Use the lightest practical setting or an unloaded movement while you learn the setup. Perform a controlled set and ask:

  • Could I repeat every rep with the same range and tempo?
  • Could I complete at least three more clean reps?
  • Did I feel stable rather than rushed or off balance?

If the answer is yes to all three, the load is reasonable for a first exposure. You can make a small increase later. If technique changes noticeably, reduce the load or get help with the setup.

How long should you rest?

Rest long enough that you can perform the next set with control. For these first sessions, roughly one to three minutes will cover many exercises, with longer rest for movements that leave you more winded. There is no prize for starting the next set while you are still unable to brace or breathe normally.

What should normal soreness feel like?

New training can produce muscle tenderness or stiffness that appears later and settles over the following days. That is different from sharp pain during a repetition, sudden loss of strength, severe swelling, or symptoms that persist or worsen. Stop the activity and seek qualified medical or training guidance when pain concerns you.

Soreness is not a score. A useful first week is one you can recover from and repeat.

What to do before each exercise

  1. Adjust the seat, pad, rack, or safety setting before loading the movement.
  2. Review the exercise demonstration and setup steps.
  3. Perform a light practice set.
  4. Choose the working load only after the movement feels controlled.
  5. Record what you completed, not what you hoped to complete.

How to progress after week one

Do not replace the whole plan just because the first week is complete. Repeat the movements long enough to improve the setup and compare like with like.

When you complete the planned reps with consistent technique and the load remains manageable, add a small amount of work next time—often one rep or the smallest practical load increase. When technique breaks down, keep or reduce the target and work on the movement.
